# The Period

It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair, we had everything before us, we had nothing before us, we were all going direct to Heaven, we were all going direct the other way--in short, the period was so far like the present period, that some of its noisiest authorities insisted on its being received, for good or for evil, in the superlative _(superlative /ˈsuːpələˌreɪtɪv/: the highest level of something; the very best or very worst)_ degree of comparison only.

There were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a plain face, on the throne of England; there were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a fair face, on the throne of France. In both countries it was clearer than crystal to the lords of the State preserves of loaves _(loaf /loʊf/: bread; used to mean “food” or “resources”)_ and fishes, that things in general were settled for ever.

It was the year of Our Lord one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Spiritual revelations _(revelation /ˌrɛvəˈleɪʃən/: something surprising that people believe comes from God)_ were conceded _(concede /kənˈsiːd/: to give or allow something)_ to England at that favoured period, as at this. Mrs. Southcott had recently attained her five-and-twentieth blessed birthday, of whom a prophetic _(prophetic /prəˈfɛtɪk/: describing something that will happen in the future)_ private in the Life Guards had heralded _(herald /ˈhɛrəld/: to announce that something important is coming)_ the sublime _(sublime /səˈblaɪm/: very beautiful or impressive)_ appearance by announcing that arrangements were made for the swallowing up of London and Westminster. Even the Cock-lane ghost had been laid only a round dozen of years, after rapping out _(rap /ræp/: to knock or tap loudly and repeatedly)_ its messages, as the spirits of this very year last past (supernaturally deficient in originality) rapped out theirs.

France, less favoured on the whole as to matters spiritual than her sister of the shield and trident _(trident /ˈtraɪdənt/: a three-pointed spear; symbol of power)_, rolled with exceeding smoothness down hill, making paper money and spending it. Under the guidance of her Christian pastors _(pastor /ˈpæstər/: a church leader or priest)_, she entertained herself, besides, with such humane achievements as sentencing a youth to have his hands cut off, his tongue torn out with pincers _(pincer /ˈpɪnsər/: a tool used to pull or hold things)_ and his body burned alive, because he had not kneeled down in the rain to do honour to a dirty procession of monks.

It is likely enough that in the rough outhouses of some tillers _(tiller /ˈtɪlər/: a person who farms or works the land)_ of the heavy lands near Paris, there were sheltered from the weather that very day, rude carts _(cart /kɑːrt/: a simple vehicle with two wheels)_ bespattered _(bespatter /bɪˈspætər/: to cover something with drops of mud or liquid)_ with rustic _(rustic /ˈrʌstɪk/: simple; from the countryside)_ mire _(mire /maɪər/: thick mud)_, snuffed _(snuff /snʌf/: to smell or sniff something)_ about by pigs, and roosted _(roost /ruːst/: for birds to sit or sleep)_ in by poultry _(poultry /ˈpoʊltri/: farm birds like chickens)_, which the Farmer, Death, had already set apart to be his tumbrils _(tumbril /ˈtʌmbrəl/: a cart used to carry people to execution)_ of the Revolution. But that Woodman and that Farmer, though they work unceasingly _(unceasingly /ʌnˈsiːsɪŋli/: without stopping)_, work silently, and no one heard them as they went about with muffled _(muffled /ˈmʌfəld/: quiet, soft, not loud)_ tread: the rather, forasmuch as to entertain any suspicion that they were awake, was to be atheistical _(atheistical /ˌeɪθiˈɪstɪkəl/: not believing in God)_ and traitorous _(traitorous /ˈtreɪtərəs/: not loyal; betraying your country)_.

In England, there was scarcely enough order and safety to justify much national boasting. Daring burglaries _(burglary /ˈbɜːrɡləri/: breaking into a building to steal things)_ by armed men, and highway robberies, took place in the capital every night; families were warned not to leave town without moving their furniture to upholsterers _(upholsterer /ʌpˈhoʊlstərər/: a person who makes or repairs soft furniture)_’ warehouses for safety; the highwayman in the dark was a City tradesman in the day, and, when recognised and challenged by another tradesman he stopped, gallantly _(gallant /ˈɡæl ənt/: bravely)_ shot him through the head and rode away.

The mail was waylaid _(waylay /ˈweɪˌleɪ/: to stop someone suddenly in order to attack them)_ by seven robbers, and the guard shot three dead, and then was shot dead himself by the other four, “in consequence of the failure of his ammunition” _(ammunition /ˌæmjəˈnɪʃən/: bullets for guns)_: after which the mail was robbed in peace.

That magnificent potentate _(potentate /ˈpoʊtənˌteɪt/: a powerful ruler)_, the Lord Mayor of London, was made to stand and deliver on Turnham Green by a single highwayman, who despoiled _(despoil /dɪˈspɔɪl/: to rob or steal)_ the important creature in sight of all his retinue _(retinue /ˈrɛtənˌjuː/: a group of helpers or followers of an important person)_; prisoners in London gaols _(gaol /ɡeɪl/: jail)_ fought battles with their turnkeys _(turnkey /ˈtɜːrnˌkiː/: jail guard)_, and the law fired blunderbusses _(blunderbuss /ˈblʌndərˌbʌs/: an old gun that fires many small metal balls)_ into them.

Thieves snipped _(snip /snɪp/: to cut something quickly)_ off diamond crosses from noble lords; musketeers _(musketeer /ˌmʌskəˈtɪər/: a soldier with a musket gun)_ went into St. Giles’s to search for contraband _(contraband /ˈkɒntrəˌbænd/: goods that are illegal to have or sell)_ goods; the mob _(mob /mɒb/: a large, angry crowd)_ fired on the musketeers, and the musketeers fired on the mob.

The hangman was in constant requisition _(requisition /ˌrɛkwɪˈzɪʃən/: needed often; regularly used)_; now hanging many types of miscellaneous _(miscellaneous /ˌmɪsəˈleɪniəs/: mixed; different kinds)_ criminals; now burning people’s hands at Newgate; now burning pamphlets _(pamphlet /ˈpæmflɪt/: a small booklet)_ at Westminster Hall; today killing an atrocious _(atrocious /əˈtroʊʃəs/: very cruel or evil)_ murderer, tomorrow a wretched _(wretched /ˈrɛtʃɪd/: very unhappy or miserable)_ pilferer _(pilferer /ˈpɪlfərər/: someone who steals small things)_ who had robbed a farmer’s boy of sixpence.

All these things happened in the year one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Surrounded by them, while the Woodman and the Farmer worked unheeded _(unheeded /ʌnˈhiːdɪd/: not noticed or ignored)_, those two of the large jaws, and those two of the plain and the fair faces, trod _(tread /trɛd/: walked)_ with enough stir, and carried their divine rights proudly. Thus the year conducted their Greatnesses, and myriads _(myriad /ˈmɪriəd/: a very large number)_ of small creatures—those in this story among them—along the roads before them.
